Architecture review — 20 August 2026

The Delivery OS proposal, measured against what THCO CRM is today

The three attached diagrams describe a seventeen-step delivery operating system with an AI layer, a client portal and an enterprise data platform underneath it. A full 34-page master specification has since been supplied and is folded in below; it confirms most of this reading and corrects one significant point — see the updated note under section 01. This is a read of the proposal against the code that is actually running: what is already built, what is genuinely new, what conflicts, and what it would cost to get from one to the other.

Current: 10 stages · 30 users · 15 projects Proposed: 17 steps · 12 named roles · 10 AI agents · 31 entities Source: backend/routers/flow.py, PROJECT_STATE.md, Master Specification V2.0
01 — The short version

What the proposal actually changes

Read the three diagrams together and they say one thing: THCO's system today is a sales pipeline with a build bucket bolted on the end. The proposal turns it into a delivery system.

Everything before a signed deal is already modelled in some form — intake, an owner, a meeting, a package, a proposal, an approval. Everything after it collapses into a single stage called "In Build (Engineering)" with a four-value status field. The proposal breaks that one stage into eleven: scoping, architecture, mockup, client iteration, POD formation, engineering, QA, UAT, handover, closure. That is the substance of the upgrade. The AI layer, the portal and the platform diagrams are real, but they are consequences and ambitions around that central change, not the change itself.

The second real change is ownership. Today a project has a "delivery owner" chosen from a dropdown. The proposal names a TSD who owns delivery end to end, and puts a named owner and named supporting roles on every one of the seventeen steps. That is a governance model, and it is the part most likely to survive contact with reality.

One thing still to settle before anyone estimates this

Crowther is confirmed as THCO's own working name for this system, not a separate entity. The master specification never names THCO across 34 pages, and its appendix cites three internal working sessions dated 18, 19 and 20 August 2026 — which briefly made the naming look like an open question. Confirmed directly (20 August 2026): this is THCO updating and bringing the new changes to itself.

Neither document has a home for the talent database. Diagram A lists "HR / Talent Systems" as an external integration, and the specification's own data model (Appendix B, 31 entities) has no candidate, resume or sourcing entity anywhere in it. Today that is not external: it is 33,547 candidates, 75,856 CV versions and roughly 21 GB of files sitting inside this application, and it is the largest thing the system holds by two orders of magnitude. If the Delivery OS is meant to replace the CRM, that has to be answered. If it is meant to sit alongside it, that is a different and much smaller project.

02 — Process

Ten stages against seventeen steps

The current pipeline lives in one dictionary, STAGES in backend/routers/flow.py. It runs 1 to 10 and does something unusual at stage 5: it splits the project into two sibling records — a proposal track (stages 6–8) and a build track (stages 9–10) that then run in parallel as separate rows in the database.

The proposal is a single sequential flow with commercial readiness sitting at step 11, between client iteration and POD formation. These two shapes are not compatible. Adopting the seventeen steps means retiring the split and merging the sibling records back together — which is a data migration on live projects, not a config change.

The shape of that tally is the finding. The gaps are not scattered — they are concentrated between the brief and the build, and between the build and the client. Discovery, architecture, design and acceptance are exactly the work a delivery consultancy is paid for, and exactly the work the current system does not see.

03 — People

The role model, and why the current one already broke

Authorisation today comes from three overlapping mechanisms: a role (super_admin, mini_admin, team_member), a set of headed units that make someone a project manager, and seven boolean flags on the user record — is_delivery_coordinator, is_delivery_owner, is_operations_owner, is_executive_approver, is_engineer, and two more.

Those flags are the closest thing the system has to the proposal's named roles, and the code records plainly that they failed. Stage 2 originally required the person named to hold is_delivery_coordinator; the requirement was removed because nobody in the company holds that flag, so the stage could not be reached at all. Stage 5 originally required is_engineer; removed for the same reason — one person holds it. The comments in flow.py say so directly.

This matters for the proposal, which asks for twelve named roles — TSD, Solution Architect, Product Designer, Security Engineer, QA, Legal, People & Ops, Finance, Senior Partner, Commercial Initiator, Engineers, Client — with an owner and supporting cast on every step. That is a stronger version of the mechanism that has already been abandoned once here. It works only if the roles are actually filled, which is an organisational question before it is a software one.

TodayTHCO CRM
  • 3 access tiers — administrator, project manager, collaborator
  • Manager rights come from two separate places (users.headed_units and units.head_user_id) and both must be read
  • 7 role flags, most of them unheld and their gates removed
  • Collaborators see the task board only — no pipeline, no tickets
  • Clients have no account and no way in
  • Identity resolution cached 45 s per session token
ProposedDelivery OS
  • 12 named roles, each with defined responsibilities
  • A single Role & Access Management service as the source of truth
  • An owner named on every one of 17 steps, plus supporting roles
  • SSO and MFA at the access layer, RBAC behind it
  • A client portal with limited access — clients act at steps 10 and 15
  • Approval Engine as a distinct service, not per-stage code
04 — Intelligence

The AI layer is the biggest genuine departure

There is AI in the current system, but it is doing something completely different. FlowForge is an automation builder: staff describe a repetitive task in conversation and it generates an n8n workflow for approval. It runs against its own Supabase database, separate from the CRM's MongoDB, and it has no connection to the delivery pipeline whatsoever.

The proposal puts AI on the critical path. Step 02 is an AI system reading the intake, scoring and ranking every TSD by expertise, domain, capacity, experience and history, then recommending an assignment. A "What Next" engine suggests the next action at every stage. A knowledge graph holds project context. Document intelligence reads what comes in. A copilot sits over all of it.

The scoring engine cannot work yet, and it is worth saying so early

Ranking TSDs "based on expertise, domain, capacity, experience & history" requires data this system does not hold. There are no skill records, no capacity records and no delivery history on any user account. There are 15 projects in production total. Even a perfect model has nothing to rank on.

That is not an argument against the feature — it is an argument about sequence. The structured data has to be captured first, through the seventeen steps, for a year or so, before an assignment engine has anything to say. Build the process; the intelligence becomes possible as a by-product.

One genuine irony worth noting: the existing CV parser, deck splitter, identity resolver and duplicate detector already constitute a working document-intelligence pipeline — they just point at candidate CVs rather than client briefs. That capability is real and reusable.

05 — Platform

Data and infrastructure: an enterprise stack for thirty people

TodayRunning in production
  • One MongoDB cluster — Cosmos DB for MongoDB (vCore), a single burstable core
  • Files live in the database. ~21 GB of CVs, task attachments and profile photos, all as documents
  • Search is Mongo's $text index
  • Caching is a 45-second in-process dict — no Redis
  • An audit_log collection, mutable like any other
  • No warehouse, no analytics store
  • One container on Azure Container Apps, one region, scaling to zero
  • GitHub Actions deploy on push. No CI test suite
  • No WAF, no IDS, no Prometheus, no ELK, no rate limiting
ProposedDiagrams D & E
  • PostgreSQL as the operational database
  • MinIO / S3 object store for documents and artefacts
  • OpenSearch as the search index
  • Redis as a shared cache
  • An immutable audit log store, separate from operational data
  • A data warehouse feeding reports and dashboards
  • Kubernetes across three availability zones, behind a WAF and load balancer
  • Prometheus / Grafana / ELK observability, automated backup and DR
  • Secrets management, compliance and governance, rate limiting as cross-cutting services

Three honest observations about that column on the right.

The Postgres move is already costed, and it is not small. docs/POSTGRES_MIGRATION.md measured it: 463 MongoDB driver call sites, 275+ query operators, 40 collections, 27 aggregation pipelines, 27 index definitions — all written inline, with no ORM and no repository layer to swap. The estimate is 6–9 weeks for one engineer, roughly doubling if feature work continues in parallel. The data itself is clean and would map well; it is the code that is welded to Mongo.

The object store is the one infrastructure change that pays for itself immediately. Storing 21 GB of files in the operational database is what made the 19 August migration heavy enough to take the cluster down — not the space, the sheer number of write operations against a single burstable core. Moving files to blob storage is a contained change with a direct reliability payoff, and it does not require Postgres, Kubernetes or anything else on that list.

The rest is sized for a company THCO is not yet. Multi-AZ Kubernetes, OpenSearch, a warehouse, WAF, IDS and a full observability stack for 30 users and 15 projects is a substantial recurring cost against a system that currently scales to zero overnight to save money. These are the right destinations. They are not the right next step.

07 — Recommendation

Take the process now, and the platform later

The most useful thing in these three diagrams is the seventeen-step process and the named owner at each step. That part is correct, overdue, and cheap — it is a dictionary, a set of validators and a set of forms in a codebase that already has a working state machine, audit trail, notification service and task board.

The platform diagrams are a different project with a different budget, and bundling them together is how this stalls. Below is the sequence I would actually run.

  1. Phase 1

    Adopt the seventeen steps on the existing stack

    Rewrite STAGES, add the per-step structured fields, extend LEGACY_STAGE_MAP so the 15 live projects land somewhere sensible, and decide what happens to the stage-5 sibling split. Most of the business value of this proposal is in this phase.

    Estimate 2–3 weeks · no new infrastructure · reversible
  2. Phase 2

    Build the missing delivery artefacts

    Discovery and scoping, solution architecture, mockup and demo, UAT sign-off, handover pack, closure report. These are forms and documents attached to a project, reusing the attachment infrastructure the task board already has. Add the architect, designer and QA roles here — but only for roles that are genuinely filled.

    Estimate 4–6 weeks · this is where the delivery gap actually closes
  3. Phase 3

    Move files out of the database

    Blob storage for CVs, attachments and artefacts. Independent of everything else on this list, and the single highest-value reliability change available — it directly addresses what caused the 19 August outage.

    Estimate 1–2 weeks · do this regardless of what else is agreed
  4. Phase 4

    Introduce a data access layer, still on MongoDB

    Collapse the 463 scattered driver calls into a repository interface. Worth doing on its own merits, and it turns the eventual Postgres move from a rewrite of the whole application into a rewrite of one layer. This is the recommendation the migration document already made.

    Estimate 3–4 weeks · prerequisite for any database decision
  5. Phase 5

    Client portal, then AI, then platform — in that order

    The portal unlocks steps 10 and 15 and is the first thing clients ever see, so it earns its place before anything internal. AI assignment waits until the seventeen steps have produced enough structured history to rank on. Kubernetes, OpenSearch, the warehouse and the observability stack wait until headcount or client exposure demands them.

    Not estimable yet · revisit once Phases 1–4 have landed

The one-line version, for whoever asks

The proposal is right about the process and ahead of itself on the platform. Adopting the seventeen steps and the named owners is a few weeks of work on the system that already exists, and it fixes the real problem — that everything after "we won the deal" is currently invisible. The AI, the portal and the enterprise data platform are worth building toward, but they should follow the process rather than arrive with it.
