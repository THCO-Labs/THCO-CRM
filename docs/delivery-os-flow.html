Delivery OS — process explainer

How a project moves through the new seventeen-step flow

The source diagrams pack the whole platform onto one sheet, which makes the process itself hard to follow. This redraws the flow, now checked against the 34-page master specification rather than the diagrams alone: who holds the project at each step, where the client comes in and out, where the one loop is, and where the specification's own gate sits. Section 07 covers what the full specification adds beyond what the diagrams show.

01 — The flow

Seventeen steps, five phases, one gate

02 — The validation gate

A demo is not permission to build

The master specification names this rule directly, twice — once as a stage gate and once as one of its twenty non-negotiables: "A demo does not automatically authorize engineering. Client validation is a formal gate into delivery preparation." Steps 01 through 10 — intake, discovery, product definition, an architect's brief, a designer's time, scoping, architecture, a working mockup, and however many rounds of client feedback it takes — all run before that gate. Step 11, Validation & Readiness, is where the client confirms final direction, scope and acceptance criteria and the project is marked CLIENT VALIDATED. Only then does step 12 — Delivery Preparation / Pod Formation — begin.

That much is unambiguous in the 34-page specification. What is not settled is whether a contract sits at that same point. The three architecture diagrams label step 11 "Validation & Commercial Readiness," own it to TSD + Legal + Finance, and list "commercial proposal, legal review, contract preparation" as its activities. The specification text, by contrast, states the opposite as a deliberate V2 change: "Commercial/finance administration is outside the core technical Delivery OS flow" — and Stage 11's owner in the spec's own lifecycle table is TSD + Client, full stop.

That is a real disagreement between two documents describing the same system, not a reading error. It changes what step 11 is: either a commercial and legal milestone, or a purely product-and-scope milestone that a signed contract sits outside of entirely. This diagram follows the specification text, since it is the newer, more explicit document — but the discrepancy needs resolving with whoever owns both documents before step 11 is built.

Whichever reading wins, two practical consequences follow and neither is a software detail:

  • Steps 01–10 need a kill switch. The flow as drawn has no exit before step 11. Something has to let a TSD or a partner stop a project at step 07 when it is clearly not going anywhere — and record why, so the pattern is visible later. The current CRM has this in crude form (mark_lost with a reason); the new flow needs it at every pre-validation step.
  • Pre-validation effort has to be measurable. If the firm is investing real delivery capacity — an architect's time, a designer's time — before a client has validated anything, someone will eventually ask what that costs and what the conversion rate is. The system should be able to answer. Today it cannot — there is no way to attribute anyone's time to a project at all.

Open, and worth resolving before Sprint 3

Is step 11 a product/scope gate only, or does it also carry the commercial proposal, legal review and contract preparation the diagrams show? The answer decides whether Legal and Finance are stakeholders in the core Delivery OS flow at all, which the spec says they are not.

04 — Walkthrough

What actually happens in each phase

Phase A 01–03 Commercial · AI · TSD

Intake & Assignment

Someone commercial — sales, BD, a partner — creates the project from a single form. Not just a name and a client: the desired outcome, the initial brief, call transcripts, supporting documents, client website and context, all captured at the point of entry rather than living in somebody's inbox.

The AI then reads all of it and scores every available TSD against the brief on expertise, domain, capacity, experience and past delivery history, ranks the best matches and recommends one. A human still confirms. The chosen TSD gets an email and an in-app notification, a project workspace is created for them automatically, and they land on a summary with recommended next steps rather than an empty record.

Produces: intake record · TSD ranking & rationale · project workspace

Phase B 04–08 TSD, then Solution Architect & Designer

Definition & Design

This is the phase that does not exist today, and it is where most of the value of the upgrade sits. The TSD reads everything, identifies the gaps, and goes back to the client with clarification questions rather than guessing. Then they write the outcome brief: the problem stated plainly, defined outcomes, success metrics, scope boundaries, initial assumptions, risks and constraints.

Only once that exists do they request a Solution Architect — a request that goes through the system, may need Senior Partner approval, and delivers the architect a proper brief rather than a conversation. The architect and a product designer then run discovery and scoping (user needs, process flows, features, effort estimate, roadmap) and produce the architecture itself (tech stack, integrations, data architecture, security and compliance, high-level estimate).

The difference from today is the difference between "we sent them a package" and "we know exactly what we are building, what it will take, and where the risk is."

Produces: outcome brief · scoping document · architecture record · effort estimate

Phase C 09–11 Architect, Designer, TSD, Client

Validate & Commit

A mockup or first demo — wireframes, a prototype, a technical walkthrough — goes in front of the client. They respond, the solution is refined, scope is updated, and the AI analyses what changed. Then it goes round again, as many times as it takes. This is the only loop in the flow and it is deliberate: the demo is the sales instrument, so it iterates until the client is convinced.

Step 11 closes the loop for good. The client confirms final direction, scope and acceptance criteria, and the project is marked CLIENT VALIDATED — the formal gate the specification requires before delivery preparation can open. Whether a contract, legal review and commercial proposal sit inside this step or outside the Delivery OS flow entirely is unresolved — see §02.

Produces: demo · feedback & change log · client-validated gate

Phase D 12–14 TSD, People & Ops, POD, QA

Build

A POD is formed — a named delivery team, with talent engaged, resources confirmed, kickoff planned and a delivery plan agreed. The POD is a real object in the new model, not just a set of people assigned to a project.

Engineering then runs conventionally: sprint planning, development, code reviews, CI/CD, documentation. QA runs as its own step with its own lead — test planning, test execution, defect tracking, and preparation for UAT. This is the part THCO already does well; the existing task board covers most of step 13 today. What is missing is QA as a distinct, owned step rather than a status value.

Produces: POD · delivery plan · built system · test plan & defect log

Phase E 15–17 Client, TSD, Architect, Finance

Accept & Close

The client runs UAT against the acceptance criteria agreed back at step 11 and signs off. Then handover: knowledge transfer, handover documentation, training, operational readiness — the client's team has to be able to run the thing without THCO in the room.

Closure is a real step, not a status flip. A closure report, lessons learned, the project archived, repositories cleaned up, final documentation filed. Lessons learned is the step that feeds everything back — it is what eventually gives the AI assignment engine at step 02 something real to rank people on.

Produces: acceptance record · handover pack · closure report · lessons learned

07 — Beyond the diagrams

What the 34-page master specification adds that the three diagrams did not show

A full written specification, Crowther_Delivery_OS_Updated_Master_Specification_V2.pdf, dated August 2026, sits behind the diagrams and goes considerably further than they do. It confirms the seventeen steps and the ownership model above almost exactly, but it also describes a second layer that the diagrams only hinted at with single boxes such as "AI Delivery Copilot" and "Knowledge Graph." Spelled out, that layer is substantial on its own.

LayerWhat the specification actually asks for
Ten AI agents Not one AI step at intake, but a named agent per concern: Project Intelligence, Product Scoping, Architecture Intelligence, Delivery, Contract Intelligence, Talent, QA, Risk, Executive, and the What Next? Engine that recommends the next action on every open project.
Thirty-one core entities Appendix B lists User, Role, Client, Stakeholder, Project, Project Stage, Stage Transition, Requirement, Feature, User Journey, Product Brief, Architect Brief, Architecture Version, Architecture Component, Integration, Decision, Risk, Dependency, Task, Milestone, Pod, Assignment, Document, Feedback Item, Notification, QA Test, Defect, Acceptance Record, Handover Record, Closure Record and Knowledge Item, each with its own relationships. Section 6 of the sprint plan lists ten new objects; the specification's own count is three times that.
Knowledge Graph and Project Memory A dedicated graph connecting client, project, requirement, decision, document, person and architecture-component entities, so AI "uses relevant historical context rather than only the latest message or document." Nothing in the current CRM resembles this, so it is new infrastructure rather than a new field.
Decision Log, immutable activity log, stage history Every consequential decision gets its own record: title, description, decision maker, participants, reason, alternatives considered, and which requirements, architecture, timeline or scope it affects. The CRM's existing audit_log collection is a reasonable start on the activity-log half of this, but the structured decision record is new.
Risk and Dependency Intelligence A formal risk register (probability, impact, severity, mitigation, contingency) and a dependency engine tracking blockers across tasks, people, documents, approvals, client actions and external systems. Not represented anywhere in the current system.
Project Control Tower A portfolio-level view of health (green/amber/red), schedule risk, blockers, scope creep, staffing gaps and QA readiness, plus an Executive Exception View that shows leadership only what needs intervention. This is a reporting surface built on top of everything else, not a step in the pipeline.
Information Continuity / Change Propagation Every new piece of information, a transcript, a message, a document, is meant to be extracted, classified as fact, change, new requirement, question or conflict, run through impact analysis against product, architecture, scope and timeline, and routed to affected owners automatically. This is the mechanism the AI agents actually run on, and it is the single largest piece of unbuilt infrastructure in the whole specification.

What this means for the sprint plan

The ten-sprint plan in docs/DELIVERY_OS_SPRINT_PLAN.md delivers the seventeen-step process, the part of the specification the diagrams communicated clearly. It does not deliver the intelligence and control layer above. That is not a gap in the plan; it is a deliberate sequencing choice the specification itself endorses in its own words, in section 46, MVP Build Sequence: "Do not attempt to build every feature simultaneously. Establish the operating architecture, build the control system, test it, then expand." The specification's own MVP 1 ("Project Control System") and part of MVP 2 ("Product + Architecture") are roughly what the ten sprints cover. MVPs 3 through 5, the demo loop's AI change analysis, the ten agents, the Knowledge Graph, and the Control Tower, are a second project, not a phase that was forgotten.
